# 1. OWNERSHIP CAN CHANGE PERCEIVED VALUE

Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler's classic market experiments found a persistent gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay for consumption goods in their experimental settings. This is commonly discussed as the **endowment effect**.

Source relationship:

`possession → changed valuation threshold`

Why it matters as source material:

Teswa begins with objects that are already owned. The owner's relationship to an item may not be equivalent to a neutral catalog seller setting a price for inventory.

Important guardrail:

Do not turn a behavioral-economics result into a claim that every Teswa user overvalues every possession. The useful source idea is simply:

> ownership itself can participate in valuation.

Potential tension:

**what it is worth to me ↔ what it is worth to you**

Source:
- Daniel Kahneman, Jack L. Knetsch, Richard H. Thaler, “Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem,” *Journal of Political Economy* 98(6), 1990. DOI: `10.1086/261737`
- https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261737

# 2. POSSESSIONS CAN PARTICIPATE IN IDENTITY

Russell Belk's “Possessions and the Extended Self” argues that possessions can contribute to and reflect identity.

Source relationship:

`person ↔ possession ↔ self-expression / memory / continuity`

Why this matters:

A personal inventory is not automatically equivalent to a merchant catalog. If Dolab survives product research, its strongest source may be the relationship between a person and the objects they keep, show, release or exchange.

This creates a more interesting question than “how should My Listings look?”

> What does a person's set of possessions communicate, and what changes when one of those possessions becomes available to someone else?

Guardrail:

Do not make every used object sentimental. Many possessions are practical, replaceable or unwanted. Identity is one possible source relationship, not the only motivation.

Source:
- Russell W. Belk, “Possessions and the Extended Self,” *Journal of Consumer Research* 15(2), 1988. DOI: `10.1086/209154`
- https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/15/2/139/1841428

# 3. USED GOODS CONTAIN INFORMATION ASYMMETRY

George Akerlof's “Market for Lemons” uses used cars as a concrete model for markets where one side has better information about quality than the other.

Source relationship:

`hidden quality → uncertainty → lower confidence / distorted market behavior`

Teswa relevance:

The owner usually knows more about an item's history, defects, use and condition than the person encountering it.

The design problem later is not merely “add a condition badge.”

The deeper product question is:

> How does evidence travel from the person who knows the object to the person who must decide whether to trust it?

Possible evidence sources to investigate later:
- photography
- video
- detail/condition description
- ownership history where appropriate
- conversation
- reputation
- consistency between claims and media

Guardrail:

Do not claim Teswa users are deceptive. Information asymmetry exists even when both parties are honest.

Source:
- George A. Akerlof, “The Market for ‘Lemons’: Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 1970.
- DOI: `10.2307/1879431`

# 4. TRUST BETWEEN STRANGERS IS MARKET INFRASTRUCTURE

Research on internet marketplaces has repeatedly treated reputation as a mechanism that helps strangers transact under uncertainty.

Resnick and Zeckhauser studied eBay's reputation system; later marketplace-design literature treats trust/reputation choices as part of the market mechanism rather than decorative social proof.

A 2021 meta-analysis across 107 peer-to-peer marketplace studies found a reputation effect across different operationalizations, while also finding meaningful variation in its size/context.

Source relationship:

`past observable behavior → reputation signal → changed willingness to interact`

Teswa implication at source level:

Profiles, reviews, block/report and deal history should not automatically be separated into a generic “social layer.” They may participate directly in the feasibility of exchange.

Important tension:

**trust signal ↔ false certainty**

A number or badge can compress evidence, but it can also hide context or bias.

Guardrail:

Do not equate “more ratings UI” with “more trust.” Later design must ask what evidence is credible, reciprocal, timely and relevant to the next action.

Sources:
- Paul Resnick & Richard Zeckhauser, “Trust Among Strangers in Internet Transactions: Empirical Analysis of eBay's Reputation System,” 2002/2004 publication lineage.
  - https://rzeckhauser.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/trust-among-strangers-internet-transactions-empirical-analysis-ebays
- Michael Luca, “Designing Online Marketplaces: Trust and Reputation Mechanisms,” *Innovation Policy and the Economy* 17, 2017. DOI: `10.1086/688845`
  - https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/688845
- Ruohuang Jiao, Wojtek Przepiorka, Vincent Buskens, “Reputation effects in peer-to-peer online markets: A meta-analysis,” *Social Science Research* 95, 2021. DOI: `10.1016/j.ssresearch.2020.102522`

# 5. THE FIRST OFFER CAN CHANGE THE NEGOTIATION FIELD

Galinsky and Mussweiler's experiments found that first offers can act as anchors and predict later settlement values in negotiation settings.

Source relationship:

`first proposal → reference point → later negotiation state`

This makes an offer more than a message payload.

An offer can alter:
- expectations
- perceived range
- what counts as movement/concession
- the meaning of the next response

Teswa source question:

> If Offers are core, how should the product preserve the causal history of a proposal rather than treating every message as equivalent chat text?

Guardrail:

Do not optimize the product to exploit anchoring or pressure users. The source value is understanding that **proposal order has consequence**.

Source:
- Adam D. Galinsky & Thomas Mussweiler, “First Offers as Anchors: The Role of Perspective-Taking and Negotiator Focus,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 81(4), 2001. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.81.4.657`
- https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/first-offers-anchors-role-perspective-taking-and-negotiator-focus

# 6. SECOND-HAND MOTIVATION IS NOT ONE THING

A 2019 study developing a scale for online second-hand shopping motivation identified several dimensions grouped around economic, convenience and ideological motivations. The reported dimensions included price orientation, bargaining power, usefulness/ease, uniqueness, nostalgia, trust and assurances.

Source relationship:

`same market → different reasons for participation`

Teswa relevance:

A person can enter a second-hand product world because they want:
- a better economic outcome
- bargaining agency
- something unusual
- a convenient route to an item
- reassurance/trust
- a specific object with history or scarcity

This is useful because a single generic “shopper persona” can flatten the product.

Guardrail:

The study does **not** prove these motivations map one-to-one onto Teswa users in Egypt. It expands the research field; local user evidence must decide what survives.

Source:
- “Online second-hand shopping motivation – Conceptualization, scale development, and validation,” *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*, 2019. DOI: `10.1016/j.jretconser.2019.05.014`
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0969698919301742

# 7. A MARKETPLACE REDUCES FRICTION, BUT TESWA SHOULD IDENTIFY WHICH FRICTION

Marketplace-design literature often describes online marketplaces as reducing transaction costs and enabling transactions that otherwise would not occur.

That phrase is too broad to design from.

For Teswa, the source field should be decomposed into concrete frictions:

- finding a relevant object
- proving its condition
- understanding what the owner wants
- discovering whether an offer is welcome
- establishing enough trust to talk
- carrying item context into conversation
- handling distance / meetup feasibility
- moving from talk to an actual deal state
- knowing what has been agreed

Source relationship:

`friction removed → previously unlikely exchange becomes possible`

Question:

> Which friction is Teswa uniquely good at removing, and which frictions are simply table stakes?

Source trail:
- Michael Luca, 2017, marketplace trust/reputation mechanisms, DOI `10.1086/688845`.

# 8. PERSONAL INVENTORY CREATES A TWO-SIDED VALUE FIELD

This is a **Teswa-derived source hypothesis**, not an academic conclusion.

If a user can both want an item and possess items that can enter offers/deals, Teswa is not only:

`money → item`

It can contain:

`what I want ↔ what I have`

That relationship is structurally different from a conventional merchant catalog.

Questions for deeper product-contract research:
- Can offers contain another item, money, or both?
- Can more than one owned item participate?
- When does Dolab become relevant to an offer?
- Is the owner's desired exchange visible before contact?
- What happens to inventory state when a deal is accepted/completed?

Do not build design language around this until the exact current contract is verified.

# 9. EXCHANGE HAS THRESHOLDS

Current native product states — item, offer, contextual/direct conversation, deal, review — suggest progression rather than one continuous feed interaction.

Source relationship:

`attention → expression of interest → proposal → clarification → commitment → outcome → reputation`

The key source property is **threshold**.

Some actions are cheap/reversible:
- view
- save/follow where applicable
- open profile

Others carry more consequence:
- make offer
- accept/reject
- establish deal
- exchange personal/logistical details
- complete
- review/report

Later UX should respect the difference between exploration and commitment.

No visual behavior is chosen yet.

# 10. OBJECT TRUTH IS MULTI-SOURCE

In a peer-to-peer used-object context, the “item” may be known through a stack of evidence rather than a single canonical catalog record:

- owner-provided media
- owner-provided description
- category/spec details
- condition
- location
- profile/reputation
- conversation
- offer/deal history
- later review/outcome

Source relationship:

`many partial signals → working confidence`

Possible deeper principle later:

**truth accumulates**

But this is not accepted yet.

The first source world contains these tensions:

- **mine ↔ yours**

- **owner value ↔ seeker value**
- **known quality ↔ uncertain quality**
- **claim ↔ evidence**
- **stranger ↔ trusted enough**
- **browse ↔ propose**
- **proposal ↔ response**
- **possibility ↔ commitment**
- **private possession ↔ public availability**
- **identity ↔ inventory**
- **discovery ↔ intent**
- **near ↔ worth the effort**
- **social signal ↔ transactional truth**
- **history ↔ current state**

These are **raw source tensions**, not a design system.
